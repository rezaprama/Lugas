# Statistical and Scientific Writing

## Statistical significance

`Statistically significant` does not mean important, large, certain, replicable, or practically meaningful. When available, report the effect estimate, interval, scale, outcome, and analysis context. Do not replace a p-value threshold with a qualitative importance claim.

## Null and imprecise results

Failure to reject a null hypothesis is not proof of no effect. Distinguish:

- evidence of absence from absence of evidence;
- an equivalence or non-inferiority analysis from an ordinary non-significant result;
- a precise estimate near zero from a wide interval compatible with meaningful effects.

## Association and causality

Use association language unless the identification design and assumptions support a causal estimand. Temporal order alone is not enough. Do not invent mechanisms or imply that adjustment removes all confounding.

## Generalisation

Keep conclusions within the sampled population, setting, time, measurement process, and design. Separate statistical generalisation from transferability in qualitative work. Do not convert a local pattern into a universal claim.

## Prediction

Prediction, explanation, and causation answer different questions. Report the evaluation population, outcome horizon, comparator, and uncertainty where known. Improved prediction does not establish mechanism or intervention benefit.

## Novelty and priority

Avoid `first`, `unprecedented`, `novel`, `definitive`, or `confirms` unless independently substantiated. A literature search can miss work; phrase the boundary and search scope when priority matters.

## Results and Discussion

Results wording should report analysis outputs without adding unsupported interpretation. Discussion wording may interpret, but should mark inference, alternatives, limitations, and evidence boundaries. Do not erase this distinction during editing.
